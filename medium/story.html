What India's medical register says about its 1.7 million doctors

I downloaded every record on the national register and turned it into charts. Here is what stood out.

Every doctor allowed to practise modern medicine in India is on a register. Each state has a medical council, and the National Medical Commission collects their lists into one national register, the Indian Medical Register. You can search it one name at a time on the NMC website. What you can't easily do is see the whole of it at once.

So I downloaded all of it: 1.73 million registrations from 29 state councils and the old central register, as of November 2026. I started with Kerala's council, the Travancore Cochin Medical Council, and then widened it to the whole country. The result is an interactive dashboard where you can pick any state: nethahussain.github.io/india-doctors-dashboard.

Here are six things I learned along the way.

1. India now registers almost six times as many doctors a year as in 1990

In 1990, 15,133 doctors joined the register. In 2025 it was 87,519. For decades the number grew slowly; then, from the mid-2000s, it took off, as new medical colleges opened and existing ones added seats.

Even that is an undercount. Several councils, Kerala's among them, stopped sending new entries to the national register years ago. Kerala's last entries are from 2019; Bihar, Haryana and Madhya Pradesh stop in 2020.

2. More than a third of all registrations are from the last ten years

The register has no date of birth for many doctors and no record of retirement or death, so once a doctor is on it, they stay on it. To get a sense of who is still working, I estimated age from the year of registration, since most Indian doctors register at about 25.

By that measure, about 647,000 registrations, more than a third, belong to doctors under 35. Around 1.40 million are under 65, the usual retirement age. India's medical workforce is young, and the bulge of new doctors is still working its way through.

3. Some states have ten times as many doctors per person as others

Across India there are about 10.8 registered doctors under 65 for every 10,000 people. That average hides enormous differences. Sikkim has 34.2, Karnataka 27.9 and Goa 27.7. At the other end, Bihar and Jharkhand have 2.8 each, and Uttar Pradesh, India's most populous state, has 4.3.

These figures count where doctors registered, not where they work. A doctor registered in Karnataka may well be working in Bihar, or in Dubai. But the gap is too large to be explained away by that. The southern states, which built many medical colleges early, register many more doctors, and many of those doctors stay.

4. The map follows the medical colleges

The map at the top of this story shows where registered doctors live, using the PIN code in their address. About 782,000 doctors have an address with a PIN code. The dots crowd along the south-west coast and across Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, and around Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, Hyderabad and Ahmedabad. Large parts of central and eastern India are thin.

One caution: in some states, Maharashtra and Rajasthan especially, few addresses on the register have a PIN code at all, so those states look emptier on the map than they are.

5. In three states, most new doctors are women

The register doesn't record gender, so I estimated it from first names, which works well for most Indian names but not all. With that caveat, a clear pattern shows up in the south. In Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Kerala, women have outnumbered men among newly registered doctors every year since the mid-2010s. In Telangana, 57% of new doctors in 2025 were women.

In 2000, women were about 40% of new doctors in Andhra Pradesh and Kerala. (Telangana's council only began in 2014.)

6. One in ten new doctors now studied abroad

About 99,000 doctors on the register earned their basic medical degree outside India. In 1990, only one in 200 new registrations came with a foreign degree. Today it is about one in ten.

The countries have changed over time. In the 1960s the largest groups studied in what is now Bangladesh and in the United Kingdom. In the 2000s it was Russia and Ukraine. In the 2010s, China. In the 2020s the Philippines has risen fast, and after the war began in Ukraine in 2022, thousands of Indian students there had to come home in the middle of their course. Altogether, China, Russia and Ukraine trained almost half of India's foreign-educated doctors. The register doesn't record nationality, but most are probably Indian students who went abroad for a seat they couldn't get, or afford, at home. Foreign graduates must pass a screening exam before they can register in India.

What the register can't tell us

A register is an administrative list, not a census, and it has blind spots:

  • It has no gender field, so gender is estimated from first names. About 11% of names couldn't be placed.
  • It doesn't say who is still practising. Doctors who have retired, emigrated or died stay on the list.
  • A doctor registered with two state councils appears twice. That's about 2.4% of records.
  • Several councils stopped sending entries to the national register years ago, so recent years are incomplete. For doctors per 10,000 people, I projected those councils forward from their own trend.
  • It shows where doctors registered, not where they work.

Even with those limits, it's the most complete picture of India's doctors that is publicly available, and it deserves to be looked at as a whole.
